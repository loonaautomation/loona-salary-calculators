How it is calculated

The European Central Bank publishes one reference rate per currency against the euro, so every conversion on this page goes through the euro: amount in euros = amount ÷ rate of the “from” currency, then result = euros × rate of the “to” currency. In one step: result = amount × (rate of target ÷ rate of source), with the euro itself counted as 1.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the rate here different from Google or my bank?

Google shows a live mid-market rate; your bank or card shows a dealing rate with its margin added (often 1–4% worse for you). This converter uses the European Central Bank's official daily reference rates — a neutral, published benchmark — dated 6 November 2026 and baked into the page, so everyone who uses it sees the same honest reference figure.

Are these exchange rates live?

No — and the page says so openly. The rates are the ECB reference rates dated 6 November 2026, stored inside the page itself; they are refreshed in periodic site updates, not minute by minute. For a holiday budget or an invoice estimate that is exactly what you need; for a large transfer, check the live quote from the service you will actually use.

Why is my currency not listed — for example PKR?

The European Central Bank publishes reference rates for around 30 major currencies only, and currencies such as the Pakistani rupee, Bangladeshi taka or Nigerian naira are not among them. This converter covers the full ECB list — the table above shows every currency included.

How does converting between two non-euro currencies work?

Through the euro, the way the ECB's own table is built: first divide by the source currency's euro rate to get euros, then multiply by the target currency's euro rate. For example, pounds to dollars goes £ → € → $, which is why the result can differ a cent or two from a direct market quote.

Can I use this rate for my invoice or accounts?

As a documented reference, yes — many businesses use ECB reference rates for invoicing and bookkeeping precisely because they are official and stable for the day. Just record the date of the rate (6 November 2026 here) alongside it, and remember it is for indication: the amount that actually arrives will follow your bank's rate on the day.

Frequently asked questions

What does “reference rate” actually mean?

It is a benchmark the ECB publishes for information — the rate at which it values currencies for its own statistics — not a price at which anyone promises to exchange money for you. No bank will deal at exactly this rate; good services come close, expensive ones add several percent.

How much do cards and transfer services typically add?

Commonly 1–4% over the reference rate, sometimes hidden entirely in the rate rather than shown as a fee. A quick check: convert here first, then compare the amount a service offers you for the same money — the difference is its real total charge.
