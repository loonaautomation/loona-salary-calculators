Germany Gross to Net, Explained

A German payslip deducts up to seven different items before you see a cent — and almost everything about it is in German. Here is the whole system in plain English: the tax classes, the four social insurances, the soli, church tax, and a fully worked €60,000 example.

In short: from a German gross salary you pay four social contributions (together roughly 20–21% of gross, each capped) plus income tax under the statutory §32a tariff — nothing up to €12,348, then a formula that rises to 42% and 45% at the top. The solidarity surcharge now affects only high earners, and church tax applies only to registered church members.

The six tax classes (Steuerklassen)

Your tax class decides how much wage tax your employer withholds each month. It does not change your final annual tax bill — classes are a withholding mechanism, settled in your annual return.

  • Class I — single, divorced or widowed. The standard single allowance.
  • Class II — single parents; an extra relief amount of €4,260 a year.
  • Class III — married, the higher earner. Uses income splitting: your income is taxed as two halves, which sharply lowers withholding.
  • Class IV — married with similar incomes; the default for couples, each taxed like class I.
  • Class V — the lower-earning partner of someone in class III; no personal allowance, so withholding is heavy.
  • Class VI — a second job; no allowance at all, the highest withholding.

Couples choose between III/V and IV/IV: III/V pays more net per month to the higher earner but usually means a mandatory annual return and a possible back-payment; IV/IV spreads it evenly.

The four social contributions

Before any tax is calculated, four insurances come out of your gross salary. Your employer pays a matching share on top (plus accident insurance, which is employer-only and never appears in your net).

The income tax itself: the §32a formula

Germany does not use stepped brackets for most incomes — §32a of the Income Tax Act defines a smooth mathematical formula. For 2026: no tax on the first €12,348 (the basic allowance); then two progressive formula zones where the marginal rate climbs from 14% to 42%; a flat 42% marginal rate from €69,879; and 45% above €277,826. Your average rate is always well below your marginal rate — at €50,000 of taxable income the tax is €10,548, an average of about 21%.

Two honest notes about any online result, including ours. First, the tax is charged on taxable income, roughly your gross minus your social contributions and the standard lump sums (€1,230 employee expenses + €36 special expenses). Second, official payroll derives that taxable income with a detailed pension-relief calculation (the BMF's programmed flow), while calculators use the lump-sum simplification. Our calculator applies the exact statutory §32a formula — verified against the official law text — to that simplified taxable income, so treat the final net as a close estimate, not a payslip.

Soli and church tax

The solidarity surcharge (Soli) is 5.5% of your income tax, but since 2021 it only starts once your annual income tax passes €20,350 (single; €40,700 joint), and a mitigation zone phases it in gradually — it takes a salary well into six figures before the full 5.5% applies. Most employees pay €0. Church tax (Kirchensteuer) is 8% of your income tax in Bavaria and Baden-Württemberg and 9% everywhere else, and only if you are a registered member of a tax-collecting church; leaving the church (Kirchenaustritt) ends it.

Worked example: €60,000 in tax class I

  • Pension 9.3% = €5,580 · Unemployment 1.3% = €780 · Health 8.75% = €5,250 · Care 1.8% (one child) = €1,080 → social contributions €12,690
  • Taxable income ≈ 60,000 − 12,690 − 1,230 − 36 = €46,044
  • Income tax (exact §32a tariff on €46,044) = €9,186 · Soli €0 · Church tax €0
  • Net: €38,124 a year = €3,177 a month

Same salary, different situations: in class III, splitting cuts the tax to €4,668 and net rises to €42,642. With 9% church tax in class I, you pay a further €826.74 and keep €37,297.26. On €45,000 in class I, the figures are €9,517.50 of contributions, €5,436 of tax and €30,046.50 net (€2,503.88 a month).

Frequently asked questions

How much of a €60,000 salary do I keep?

In class I with no church tax: €38,124 a year (€3,177 a month). In class III: €42,642, because splitting cuts the income tax from €9,186 to €4,668.

What are the four social contributions?

Pension 9.3%, unemployment 1.3%, health about 8.75% (at the 2026 average supplementary rate) and long-term care 1.8–2.4% — each capped, roughly 20–21% of gross in total.

Do I still pay the solidarity surcharge?

Probably not: it only starts above €20,350 of annual income tax (2026, single), with a phase-in zone. Most employees pay none.

Why is my payslip slightly different from calculators?

Payroll uses the tax office's detailed programmed flow to derive taxable income; calculators use the standard lump-sum simplification on top of the exact §32a tariff. Small differences are normal — the structure and the big numbers match.
