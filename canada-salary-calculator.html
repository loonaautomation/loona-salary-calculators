How it is calculated

  • Federal tax (2026): 14% to $58,523; 20.5% to $117,045; 26% to $181,440; 29% to $258,482; 33% above. The 14% rate (cut from 15% in 2025) also values the credits: basic personal amount $16,452, Canada employment amount up to $1,500, plus your base CPP contributions and EI premiums. (The federal basic personal amount phases down at very high incomes; this model applies the full amount.)
  • CPP: 5.95% of earnings between $3,500 and $74,600 (maximum $4,230.45). The base 4.95% portion earns a tax credit; the 1.00% enhancement portion is deducted from income. CPP2: a further 4% on earnings between $74,600 and $85,000 (maximum $416), also deductible.
  • EI: 1.63% of insurable earnings up to $68,900 (maximum premium $1,123.07).
  • Ontario tax (2026): 5.05% to $53,891; 9.15% to $107,785; 11.16% to $150,000; 12.16% to $220,000; 13.16% above — less credits at 5.05% on the $12,989 Ontario basic personal amount, base CPP and EI. Then the Ontario surtax: 20% of Ontario tax above $5,818 plus 36% above $7,446; then the Ontario tax reduction (basic $316) for lower tax bills; then the Ontario Health Premium, $0–$900 on a banded scale by income ($750 at this example's salary).

Worked example: on $75,000.00 in Ontario — CPP $4,246.45 (base CPP on $71,100 of pensionable earnings plus $16.00 CPP2), EI $1,123.07, federal tax $8,258.74, Ontario tax and health premium $4,196.58 — net $57,175.17 a year.

Frequently asked questions

How much tax do I pay on a $75,000 salary in Ontario in 2026?

About $12,455 of income tax in this model: $8,258.74 federal plus $4,196.58 Ontario (including the $750 health premium), and a further $5,369.52 of CPP and EI. Net pay is about $57,175 a year — roughly 76% of gross. Your exact payslip depends on TD1 credits and pay frequency.

What is CPP2?

CPP2 is the second, additional Canada Pension Plan tier that started in 2024. Once your earnings pass the main CPP ceiling ($74,600 in 2026) you pay a further 4% on earnings up to $85,000 — a maximum of $416 in 2026. It builds a slightly larger CPP pension and, unlike base CPP, is deducted from income rather than credited.

Why does this calculator only model Ontario?

Because every province and territory runs its own tax scale, credits and surtaxes on top of the same federal system, and a single honest model beats ten sloppy ones. Ontario is Canada's largest province and the stated default in the brief for this page. Quebec differs most: it uses the QPP instead of CPP and its own tax return.

What is the Ontario Health Premium?

A separate levy collected through payroll tax in Ontario, banded by income from $0 (at $20,000 or less) to $900 (above $200,600). It is not part of the Ontario tax scale, which is why this calculator shows it inside the Ontario line and names it in the breakdown.

Are CPP and EI deducted all year?

No — both have annual maximums ($4,230.45 CPP + $416 CPP2, and $1,123.07 EI in 2026). Higher earners hit the caps part-way through the year, so late-year payslips are noticeably larger. Annual totals, as shown here, are unaffected.

How it works — the formula

Net = gross − federal tax − Ontario tax − health premium − CPP − EI. Federal and Ontario taxes are each computed on their own five-band scale, then reduced by credits valued at each system's lowest rate (14% federal, 5.05% Ontario) on the basic personal amounts, the Canada employment amount (federal only), base CPP and EI. Ontario then adds its surtax (20%/36% above the thresholds), subtracts the $316 tax reduction, and the banded Ontario Health Premium ($0–$900) is added. CPP is 5.95% between the $3,500 exemption and the $74,600 ceiling, CPP2 adds 4% up to $85,000, and EI is 1.63% up to $68,900 of insurable earnings.

Worked example

Gross $75,000.00, Ontario, 2026. Pensionable earnings $71,100 (=$74,600 − $3,500) → CPP $4,230.45; CPP2 on $400 above the ceiling → $16.00; total CPP $4,246.45. EI = 1.63% × $68,900 = $1,123.07. Federal taxable income is $74,273 after the CPP enhancement deduction; scale tax $11,421.97 minus $3,163.23 of credits = $8,258.74. Ontario scale tax $4,652.97 minus $890.39 of credits = $3,762.58, no surtax at this level, minus the $316 tax reduction = $3,446.58, plus the $750 health premium = $4,196.58. Net = $57,175.17 a year, $4,764.60 a month.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my first payslip of the year smaller than my last?

CPP and EI restart every January. Until you reach the annual maximums ($4,646.45 combined CPP/CPP2 and $1,123.07 EI in 2026) both are deducted in full; after the caps, those deductions stop and net pay jumps.

Does this include the Canada child benefit or GST credit?

No — those are benefits paid to you based on family income, not deductions from pay. They can add thousands a year for families but do not change your payslip.

I live in Quebec — how different is it?

Very. Quebec employees pay QPP (a slightly higher rate than CPP), a QPIP premium, and Quebec provincial tax on its own scale with a higher basic personal amount, and file a separate Quebec return. Treat this Ontario model as a rough guide only.

What about RRSP or pension deductions?

Workplace pension (RPP) and RRSP contributions reduce taxable income, so they lower the tax lines but also lower take-home by the amount contributed. They are personal choices, so they are not in this general model — the tax saving is roughly your marginal rate times the contribution.
