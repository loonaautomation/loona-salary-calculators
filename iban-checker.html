How it is calculated

Every IBAN follows ISO 13616. The validation used by banks — and by this checker — has four steps:

  • Normalise: remove spaces and convert to uppercase.
  • Structure: the number must match two letters (country), two check digits, then letters and digits only.
  • Length: the total length must equal the fixed length for that country — for example 22 for Germany, 27 for France, 22 for the United Kingdom, 21 for Switzerland and 15 for Norway.
  • Mod-97 checksum: move the first four characters to the end, convert letters to numbers (A = 10, B = 11, … Z = 35), then divide by 97 in chunks. Formula: rearranged IBAN mod 97 = 1 for a valid IBAN.

Frequently asked questions

What is mod-97 and how does it check an IBAN?

Mod-97 is the checksum built into every IBAN under ISO 13616. The first four characters are moved to the end, letters are converted to numbers (A=10 to Z=35), and the resulting long number is divided by 97 in chunks. A genuine IBAN always leaves a remainder of exactly 1; any other remainder means a digit or letter was typed wrongly.

What is the difference between an IBAN and a SWIFT/BIC code?

An IBAN identifies one specific bank account in one country. A SWIFT/BIC code identifies the bank and branch itself, not your account. International transfers usually need the IBAN for the account and may also ask for the bank's SWIFT/BIC.

Does a valid IBAN mean the account exists?

No. A valid result means only that the format and the mod-97 checksum are correct for that country. It cannot confirm that the account exists, is open, belongs to the person you expect, or has any money in it. Always confirm account details with the recipient through a trusted channel before sending money.

Where can I find my own IBAN?

On your bank statement, inside your banking app or online banking (usually under account details), on some bank cards, or by asking your bank. In most European countries the IBAN is printed at the top of every statement.

How it works — the formula

An IBAN is self-checking by design. Its third and fourth characters are check digits chosen by the issuing bank so that the whole number passes one exact arithmetic test. To run that test, take the IBAN, move its first four characters (country code plus check digits) to the end, replace every letter with a two-digit number (A = 10 through Z = 35), and compute that very long number modulo 97 — in practice in small chunks, carrying each remainder into the next chunk so the numbers stay manageable. If the final remainder is exactly 1, the IBAN is correctly formed; any typing error in a digit or letter changes the remainder and is caught before money moves.

Frequently asked questions

Can one wrong digit still pass the mod-97 test?

Almost never for a single mistake. The mod-97 checksum is designed to catch all single-digit errors and almost all swapped neighbouring digits, which is why banks reject a mistyped IBAN immediately instead of sending money to the wrong account.

How long is an IBAN in each country?

The length is fixed per country, from 15 characters in Norway to 31 in Malta. Germany uses 22, France 27, the United Kingdom 22, Spain 24 and Switzerland 21. This checker compares your IBAN against the exact official length for its country code before running the checksum.

Should I type my IBAN with spaces or without?

Either works here. IBANs are often printed in blocks of four for reading, but the spaces are not part of the number. This checker removes spaces automatically and shows you the print-friendly grouped version.
