Germany Tax Classes, Explained and Compared

Every employee in Germany sits in one of six tax classes (Steuerklassen I–VI), and the class on your payslip decides how much of your salary you actually see each month. Here are all six compared side by side — who qualifies, what each one does to monthly net pay, how married couples choose between III/V, IV/IV and the factor method, and how to change your class.

In short: your tax class changes only how much wage tax (Lohnsteuer) your employer withholds each month — never your final annual tax bill, which the tax return settles. Class I is for single people, II adds a €4,260 relief for single parents, III gives married higher earners the lowest withholding through income splitting, IV is the married default, V is III's high-withholding partner class, and VI covers second jobs with no allowance. What your class does — and does not — change

Wage tax is an advance payment. Your employer withholds it using your class, but your final income tax for the year is set by the statutory §32a tariff and by whether you are assessed singly or jointly — not by the class. If too much was withheld you get a refund; if too little, you pay the difference. Two consequences follow. First, a “better” class never saves tax over the year; it improves monthly cash flow. Second, combinations that withhold very little during the year (above all III/V) usually come with a mandatory annual tax return and a possible back-payment, while IV/IV without a factor usually makes filing voluntary.

Your class is stored in the ELStAM database, which your employer downloads when you start a job. It is printed on every payslip — if the class there does not match your life situation, the numbers in the table above show what that mistake can cost per month.

Married couples: III/V vs IV/IV, with worked examples

When you marry, both partners are placed in class IV automatically. You then have three choices: stay in IV/IV, switch to III/V (higher earner in III, lower earner in V), or keep IV/IV and add the factor method. Which pays more per month depends almost entirely on the gap between your two incomes.

Example 1 — one income. One partner earns €60,000, the other has no employment income (2026, no church tax). In class III, splitting cuts the wage tax to €4,668 and the household keeps €42,642 a year (€3,553.50 a month). In class IV the same salary keeps €38,124 (€3,177 a month). III therefore puts €4,518 more per year — €376.50 a month — into the household account during the year. (These are the same figures as our Germany gross-to-net guide; the annual return then settles both routes to the same final tax.)

Example 2 — two equal incomes. Both partners earn €48,000 (two children, no church tax, Zusatzbeitrag 2.9%). Combined over the year, III/V pays out €63,386 while IV/IV pays out €63,568 — with equal incomes III/V actually pays €182 less, because the class V partner's heavy withholding outweighs the class III advantage. The return settles both to the same final tax.

Rule of thumb: the bigger the income gap, the more III/V helps monthly cash flow; the closer your incomes, the better IV/IV (or IV/IV with factor) fits. The factor method: IV/IV with factor (Faktorverfahren)

The factor method is the middle route most couples overlook. Both partners stay in class IV, and the Finanzamt works out a factor — a number below 1, based on your expected joint income tax — which is applied to each partner's withholding. The splitting advantage is then shared across both payslips in proportion to income, instead of being concentrated on one partner as in III/V. Monthly net tracks the final bill closely, so neither a big refund nor a big back-payment is expected. The trade-offs: you must apply for it at the Finanzamt, the factor is valid for up to two years, and filing an annual tax return becomes mandatory.

How to change your tax class

  1. Pick the combination using the examples above or the comparison calculator — remember the annual total does not change, only the monthly split.
  2. Apply together (both spouses sign) to your local Finanzamt with the form “Antrag auf Steuerklassenwechsel” — in person, by post, or online via ELSTER. The same route applies for the factor method and for single parents applying for class II.
  3. Mind the timing: the new class takes effect from the month after your application. Since 2020 you may change more than once in a year; an application filed by 30 November still counts for that year.
  4. Check the next payslip: your employer picks the new class up automatically from ELStAM — confirm the Steuerklasse printed on the payslip has changed.

Life events change classes too, sometimes automatically: marriage puts both partners in IV; a spouse's death gives the survivor class III for the year of death and the year after, then class I; after a permanent separation, class I applies from the following year. Having a child alone does not change your class — class II is for single parents who meet its conditions.

What may change: the planned end of III/V

German government reform plans would phase out the III/V combination and move couples to IV/IV with factor instead, with a transition discussed for around 2030. As of late 2026 this is a plan, not enacted law: III/V remains fully available, and no couple needs to act now. If it passes, the factor method explained above becomes the default way to share the splitting advantage — one more reason to understand it today.

Frequently asked questions

What are the six German tax classes and who belongs in each?

Class I: single, divorced or widowed employees. Class II: single parents, with an extra €4,260 relief. Class III: the higher-earning married partner (lowest withholding, via income splitting). Class IV: married partners with similar incomes — the default. Class V: the lower-earning partner of someone in class III (highest married withholding). Class VI: a second or further job, with no allowance.

How do I find out which tax class I am in?

Your class is printed on every payslip (look for “Steuerklasse” or “Lohnsteuerklasse”) and stored in the ELStAM database that your employer downloads when you start a job. If it looks wrong — for example you married but are still in class I — your local tax office (Finanzamt) can correct it.

Which is better for married couples: III/V or IV/IV?

It depends on the gap between your incomes. The bigger the gap, the more III/V raises your combined monthly net during the year — a single earner on €60,000 keeps €4,518 a year more in class III than in class IV. With similar incomes IV/IV is usually as good or better: two partners on €48,000 each take home €182 a year more under IV/IV than under III/V. Either way, the annual tax return settles both combinations to the same final tax.

What is the factor method (IV/IV with factor)?

Both partners stay in class IV, and the tax office multiplies each partner's withholding by a factor (a number below 1) calculated from your expected joint tax. It spreads the splitting advantage across both payslips in proportion to income, so monthly net is close to the final bill and a large back-payment is unlikely. You apply for it at the Finanzamt, it is valid for up to two years, and an annual tax return is mandatory.

How do I change my tax class?

Married couples apply together to their local Finanzamt using the form “Antrag auf Steuerklassenwechsel” (application for a tax-class change), in person, by post or online via ELSTER. The new combination takes effect from the month after you apply. Since 2020 you may change more than once a year; to count for the current year, apply by 30 November. Single parents apply for class II the same way, and a change into class I after a separation is also made at the Finanzamt.

Does my tax class change how much tax I finally pay?

No. Your class only changes how much wage tax is withheld from each monthly payslip — an advance payment. Your final income tax for the year is fixed by the §32a tariff and your joint or single assessment, and the annual return settles the difference as a refund or a back-payment. Couples in III/V or using the factor method must file that return; in IV/IV without a factor, filing is usually voluntary.

Which tax class applies to a second job?

Class VI. A second and every further employment is taxed in class VI with no basic allowance, so withholding is the highest of all classes — that is deliberate, because your allowance is already used in your main job (classes I–V). The annual return settles the total across all jobs.

When does my tax class change automatically?

When you marry, both partners are placed in class IV automatically. If your spouse dies, you move to class III for the year of death and the following year, then to class I. After a permanent separation you are taxed in class I from the following year. Having a child does not change your class on its own — class II for single parents requires its conditions to be met and, where it is not set automatically, an application to the Finanzamt.
