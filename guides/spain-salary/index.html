Salary in Spain, Explained

A Spanish payslip looks simple — two deductions and a net figure — but behind it sit a capped social security system, a two-part income tax shared between Madrid and your region, and a 14-payments tradition that confuses almost every newcomer. Here is the whole system in plain English, with a fully worked €30,000 example.

In short: from a Spanish gross salary you pay employee social security of 6.50% (permanent contract, 2026 rates) plus income tax (IRPF) on a progressive scale that runs from 19% to 47% on the standard scale — after a €2,000 expenses deduction and a €5,550 personal minimum. A €30,000 gross salary keeps €23,124 a year on the standard scale. Whether you are paid in 12 or 14 instalments changes only the timing, never the annual net.

The two deductions on every payslip

Every employee in Spain has exactly two things taken off gross pay:

  • Social security (Seguridad Social) — your contribution to pensions, unemployment cover, sick and parental leave and training. It is a flat percentage of your contribution base, charged before tax.
  • IRPF withholding — personal income tax, withheld by your employer each month as an advance payment. Your real tax bill is settled later in the annual tax return (la Renta), so you may get a refund or a small top-up bill the following spring.

Your employer also pays social security on top of your salary — roughly another 30% — but that never touches your payslip net. Only the two deductions above stand between gross and net.

Social security: the 6.50% you always pay

On a permanent contract (indefinido) your share in 2026 is 6.50% of your contribution base (2026 contribution order, Orden PJC/297/2026):

On most temporary contracts the unemployment rate is 1.60%, so the total is 6.55%. The contribution base is your monthly pay including a prorated share of your extra payments. It has a floor (about €17,094 a year at the lowest band in 2026) and a cap of €5,101.20 a month — €61,214.40 a year: earnings above the cap pay no further employee contributions, which is why high earners keep a bigger share of each extra euro. For reference, the statutory minimum wage (SMI) in 2026 is €1,221 a month in 14 payments — €17,094 a year.

IRPF: the income tax, explained

IRPF is progressive: your income is sliced into bands and each slice is taxed at its own rate. The standard (common-territory) scale is:

Taxable income sliceCombined rate
Up to €12,45019%
€12,450 – €20,20024%
€20,200 – €35,20030%
€35,200 – €60,00037%
€60,000 – €300,00045%
Above €300,00047%

Three things happen before the scale is applied. First, your social security contributions come off your gross. Second, a standard €2,000 employment-expenses deduction comes off. Third, after the scale produces a tax figure, the tax on the €5,550 personal minimum (€1,054.50 at the 19% first rate) is subtracted — that minimum is the slice of income Spain treats as tax-free for everyone, and it rises with age and family circumstances.

The scale itself is really two scales added together: a state half, identical across Spain, and a regional half set by your autonomous community (see below). What your employer withholds each month is an estimate based on your expected annual pay and the family details you declare on Modelo 145; the annual Renta return corrects it. That is why two people on the same salary can see slightly different withholding — children, a partner's income and disability all feed into the estimate.

12 or 14 payments? The pagas extraordinarias

Many Spanish contracts pay the same annual salary in 14 instalments: twelve monthly payments plus two extra payments (pagas extraordinarias), usually in June or July and at Christmas. Other contracts prorate the extras into 12 equal payments. Your annual gross, tax and net are identical either way — only the rhythm changes.

One detail surprises newcomers: the two extra payments arrive without a social security deduction. That is not a bonus — contributions are charged every month on a base that already includes a prorated share of the extras, so by the time an extra payment lands, its contributions have already been paid month by month. IRPF withholding, by contrast, is taken from the extras too.

Your region changes your tax

Because each autonomous community sets its own regional half of IRPF, the same salary nets differently across Spain. Combined top rates run from about 45% in Madrid to around 50% in Catalonia and about 54% in the Valencian Community, and regional personal allowances differ too. On a €50,000 salary the gap between the cheapest and most expensive region can approach €900 a year. The figures in this guide use the standard scale — treat them as your baseline, and expect a somewhat lower net in a high-tax community.

Two territories are outside this system entirely: the Basque Country and Navarra run their own foral tax systems, with their own scales and rules. If you work there, neither this guide's scale nor a standard calculator applies.

Moved to Spain for work? The Beckham law in brief

If you have recently relocated to Spain for work, you may be able to opt into the special regime of Article 93 of the IRPF Law — the “Beckham law”. Instead of the progressive scale it charges a flat 24% on employment income up to €600,000 a year (47% above), for the year you become resident plus the five following years. The main conditions: no Spanish tax residence in the previous five tax years, a qualifying work-related move, and an application on Modelo 149 within six months of your Social Security registration. Social security is still due in full, and the regime gives no personal minimum or expenses deduction — which is why below roughly €55,000–€60,000 the standard scale is often cheaper. Worked example: €30,000, permanent contract, standard scale

  • Social security: €30,000 × 6.50% = €1,950
  • Taxable base: 30,000 − 1,950 − 2,000 = €26,050
  • Scale tax: €12,450 at 19% (€2,365.50) + €7,750 at 24% (€1,860) + €5,850 at 30% (€1,755) = €5,980.50
  • Less the personal minimum: €5,980.50 − €1,054.50 = IRPF €4,926
  • Net: €30,000 − €1,950 − €4,926 = €23,124 a year

Paid in 12 instalments that is €1,927 a month. Paid in 14, each ordinary month nets €1,628.50 (gross €2,142.86 − €351.86 IRPF − €162.50 social security) and each extra payment nets €1,791.00 (no social security deducted) — the same €23,124 over the year.

Frequently asked questions

How is net salary calculated in Spain?

Gross minus employee social security (6.50% of the contribution base on a permanent contract), then minus IRPF — worked out on the standard scale over (gross − social security − €2,000), less the tax on the €5,550 personal minimum.

How much tax do I pay on a €30,000 salary in Spain?

On the standard scale: €1,950 of social security and €4,926 of IRPF, keeping €23,124 a year — €1,927 a month over 12 payments. Your region's own scale can move the IRPF figure up or down.

What do employees pay to Social Security in Spain in 2026?

6.50% on a permanent contract: 4.70% common contingencies, 1.55% unemployment, 0.10% vocational training and 0.15% MEI. On most temporary contracts unemployment is 1.60%, making 6.55% in total. Contributions stop at the maximum base of €5,101.20 a month (€61,214.40 a year).

Why is my Spanish salary paid in 14 payments?

Many contracts split the same annual pay into 12 monthly payments plus two extras, usually in June or July and at Christmas. Annual net is identical; the extras arrive without a social security deduction because contributions are already charged monthly on a base that includes their prorated share.

Is IRPF the same in every region of Spain?

No. The state half is the same everywhere, but each autonomous community sets its regional half — combined top rates run from about 45% (Madrid) to about 54% (Valencian Community). The Basque Country and Navarra run separate foral systems.

What is the Beckham law and who can use it?

A special regime (Article 93 LIRPF) for people moving to Spain for work: flat 24% on employment income up to €600,000 (47% above), for the arrival year plus five more. You must not have been tax resident in Spain in the previous five tax years, and you apply with Modelo 149 within six months of Social Security registration. Below roughly €55,000–€60,000 the standard scale is often cheaper.
